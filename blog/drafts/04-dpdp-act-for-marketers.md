# DPDP Act for Marketers: What Changes for Your Leads, Lists and Ads

For years, many Indian businesses treated customer data casually. Phone numbers were collected at events and added to WhatsApp groups. Excel sheets of leads were shared between vendors. Email lists were bought, sold and "borrowed".

That era is ending. The **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** (DPDP Act), together with the **DPDP Rules, 2025** notified in November 2025, sets out how businesses must collect, use, store and delete personal data. Most of the obligations come into force in phases over roughly 18 months from notification, which gives businesses time to prepare. The time to start is now.

This guide explains what the DPDP Act means for everyday marketing, in plain English.

*Please note: this article is for general information. It is not legal advice. For decisions specific to your business, speak with a qualified legal professional.*

## The key terms, simply explained

- **Personal data:** any data about an individual who can be identified by it. Names, phone numbers, email addresses, photos, location data and purchase history all count.
- **Data principal:** the person the data belongs to, such as your customer or lead.
- **Data fiduciary:** the business that decides why and how personal data is used. If you run campaigns using customer data, you are a data fiduciary.
- **Data processor:** someone who handles data on your behalf, such as an agency, CRM provider or email platform.

One important point: as the data fiduciary, your business stays responsible even when an agency or tool handles the data for you.

## What the DPDP Act requires, in marketing terms

### 1. Valid consent

Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given through a clear affirmative action. In practice:

- no pre-ticked boxes
- no hiding marketing consent inside long terms and conditions
- no "by using this site you agree to everything" banners
- separate consent for separate purposes where reasonable, such as "contact me about my enquiry" versus "send me offers"

### 2. A clear notice

When you ask for consent, you need to tell people, in clear and plain language, what data you are collecting, why, and how they can withdraw consent or complain. The notice should be understandable on its own, not buried in legal text.

### 3. Easy withdrawal

People must be able to withdraw consent as easily as they gave it. If someone joined your list with one click, they should be able to leave with one click or a simple reply such as "STOP".

### 4. Purpose limitation and deletion

Use data only for the purpose you collected it for. When that purpose is served and the person is no longer engaging, the data should be erased unless the law requires you to keep it.

That old list of 20,000 leads from a 2019 expo? It is time to review it.

### 5. Security safeguards and breach reporting

You must take reasonable security measures to protect personal data. If a breach happens, you must inform the Data Protection Board and the affected individuals. Penalties for failing to protect data can go up to ₹250 crore per instance, so this is not a box-ticking exercise.

### 6. Extra care with children's data

Processing the personal data of anyone under 18 generally requires verifiable consent from a parent or guardian. Tracking and targeted advertising aimed at children are restricted. Coaching classes, schools, toy brands and kids' apps should pay close attention here.

### 7. Rights of individuals

People can ask to access a summary of their data, correct it, erase it and raise grievances. You need a clear contact point and a process to respond.

## What this means for common marketing activities

### Website lead forms

Add a short consent line and a link to your privacy notice. Use an unticked checkbox for marketing communication, separate from the enquiry itself.

Example wording:

> ☐ I agree to receive updates and offers from [Business Name] by email and WhatsApp. I can unsubscribe anytime.

### WhatsApp broadcasts and groups

Only message people who have opted in. Do not add numbers to groups without permission. Always include an easy opt-out. Our [WhatsApp marketing guide] covers how to do this without losing reach.

### Email marketing

Stop buying or renting lists. Build your own list through useful content, lead magnets and events. Keep proof of when and how each person subscribed.

### Paid ads and custom audiences

Uploading customer lists to Meta or Google for custom audiences uses personal data. Make sure your notice covers this use and that you have a valid basis for it.

### Events and offline data

Paper forms and visiting cards collected at exhibitions are personal data too. Add a consent line to the form and store the data securely.

### Working with agencies and vendors

Have written agreements that cover what data is shared, how it is used, how it is protected and when it is deleted. Ask your vendors what security they use.

## The opportunity: first-party data done right

It is easy to see the DPDP Act as a burden. We see it differently. The businesses that win in the next few years will be the ones with strong **first-party data**: information people chose to share with them directly.

People share data with brands they trust. If your forms are honest, your messages are useful and your opt-out is easy, you build a smaller but far more valuable audience. Engagement goes up, complaints go down and your sender reputation improves.

## A 10-point DPDP readiness checklist for marketers

1. List every place you collect personal data: website, WhatsApp, events, ads, billing.
2. Write a clear, simple privacy notice and link it wherever you collect data.
3. Remove pre-ticked consent boxes from all forms.
4. Separate service communication from marketing consent.
5. Add a simple opt-out to every marketing channel.
6. Record when and how each person gave consent.
7. Review old lists and delete data you no longer have a valid reason to keep.
8. Limit who in your team can access customer data.
9. Sign data agreements with agencies, CRM and software vendors.
10. Set up a contact email for privacy requests and a process to respond.

## The bottom line

The DPDP Act asks businesses to treat customer data with the same care they would want for their own. For honest marketers, that is not a big change in values, just a change in discipline. Start with your forms and your lists, and you will be well ahead of most of your competitors.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the DPDP Act apply to small businesses?**
Yes. The Act applies to businesses that process digital personal data in India, regardless of size. It also covers personal data collected offline and later digitised, such as paper forms entered into a spreadsheet.

**When does the DPDP Act come into force?**
The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025 with a phased timeline. Most core obligations apply around 18 months after notification. Check MeitY's official notifications for the latest dates.

**Can I still send marketing messages on WhatsApp and email?**
Yes, if people have given valid consent and can easily opt out. The Act does not ban marketing; it requires it to be based on clear permission.

**Do I need to delete my old customer lists?**
Review them. If you cannot show valid consent or a continuing legitimate reason to use the data, you should consider deleting it or asking those customers to opt in again.
